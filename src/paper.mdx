  <div className="wide"><ResearchFigure src={pipeline} alt="HOIST pipeline: VR demonstrations initialize a vision-language-action policy, and reinforcement learning steers initial noise while the policy and whole-body controller remain frozen." description="VR demonstrations provide the initial behavior. Ego-view RGB, side-view RGB, language and navigation commands condition the vision-language backbone. Robot state and depth are encoded for the flow-matching action expert, which outputs a 24-step chunk of 25-dimensional planner commands. A frozen whole-body controller executes head, bimanual hand, navigation and base-height targets. During reinforcement learning, a SAC actor steers initial noise using batches of 10 autonomous rollouts and terminal position and yaw rewards; the VLA and controller remain frozen." /></div>
  <div className="method-explanation">
    <h3>What the figure shows</h3>
    <p><strong>A person shows the task (0).</strong> Wearing a VR headset, an operator controls the humanoid to guide the hanging box onto its target. We record 50 of these demonstrations.</p>
    <p><strong>The robot learns to copy (1–2).</strong> An AI model reads camera images, a written instruction, and navigation commands. It combines them with the robot’s own state and a depth view to plan its next movements, and is trained to match what the operator did.</p>
    <p><strong>A controller carries out the plan (3).</strong> The plan sets targets for the head, hands, walking, and body height. An existing whole-body controller turns those targets into balanced motion and is never changed.</p>
    <p><strong>Practice improves placement (4).</strong> Copying gives a safe start but does not aim directly for accurate placement. So the robot tries the task in batches of 10, and each try is scored on how far the load settled from its target position and angle. Reinforcement learning uses those scores to replace the random starting input of the plan with a better one. The copied skill and the controller stay fixed.</p>
  </div>
